\section*{Abstract}\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}
Medical vision-language models can produce fluent, correct-looking answers without using the clinical visual evidence those answers require. The \emph{Trustworthy} thread of this dissertation tests how models use evidence; the \emph{Efficient} thread addresses the resources needed to train and evaluate them and to make predictions. Studies of evidence alignment use radiology report structure and clinician gaze to guide image representations while examining parameter sharing, limited supervision, and energy use. A separate, non-medical study shows that a model can synthesize visual features internally as needed during reasoning, using fewer perceptual tokens while retaining accuracy in a reported comparison; its application to medical imaging remains to be tested. An audit detects silent failures, in which a model answers confidently after required evidence is removed or corrupted, and extends the tests to three-dimensional scans with automatically computed labels. A further study tests whether a clinical concept recoverable from a model's internal representation also controls its answer, finding that on chest radiographs it often does not. Proposed work examines two ways to connect answers to localized evidence: one derives a spatial answer from a point selected by the model; the other flags regions that a system trained on normal or unlabeled scans cannot predict from their surroundings as candidate abnormalities, then uses a separately trained model to identify them. The latter approach investigates performance with limited lesion labels, and both methods require further validation.
